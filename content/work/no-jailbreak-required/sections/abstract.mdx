Language models are now used in health care and public information, and each provider trains its own model to refuse harmful requests. That independence creates a weakness: if one model complies with a harmful request that the others refuse, the refusals protect no one, because anyone can send the same request to the model that complies. We audited refusals across seven frontier models (<ModelPill model="openai" label="GPT-4o" />, <ModelPill model="claude" label="Claude 3.7 Sonnet" />, <ModelPill model="gemini" label="Gemini 2.5 Pro" />, <ModelPill model="grok" label="Grok-3" />, <ModelPill model="deepseek" label="DeepSeek-V3" />, <ModelPill model="qwen" label="Qwen3-235B" /> and <ModelPill model="llama" label="Llama 3.3 70B" />) on 100 harmful requests from the AdvBench benchmark, asked plainly, with no system prompt and no jailbreak. Each model on its own complied with only 1 to 3% of requests. But 8% of requests were answered by at least one model while others refused (7% without one borderline request). We call these the exploitable gap: requests that look safely handled across the ecosystem, but that anyone can get answered by switching providers. No request was answered by all seven models, so safety training does agree on some harms. The results argue for coordination on safety across providers, and for refusal benchmarks that measure the whole ecosystem, not one model at a time.
