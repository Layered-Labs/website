Large language models are being proposed for clinical decision support, including mass-casualty triage, where who is treated first must depend only on how badly they are hurt. We tested whether two models keep their triage decisions the same when nothing changes but the patient's stated education level, a detail that should play no part in disaster triage. We took the 87 vignettes of the TRIAGE benchmark, added one sentence giving the patient's education, kept every clinical detail the same, and asked <ModelPill model="qwen" label="Qwen-2.5-72B" /> and <ModelPill model="openai" label="GPT-4o-mini" /> to triage each version. Overall accuracy barely moved: <ModelPill model="qwen" label="Qwen-2.5-72B" /> scored 0.644 under every condition, and <ModelPill model="openai" label="GPT-4o-mini" /> between 0.598 and 0.644. But both models changed individual decisions. <ModelPill model="qwen" label="Qwen-2.5-72B" /> changed its triage for 5.75% of patients with the lowest education cue and 10.34% with the highest, and its changes leaned toward less urgent care; two of the three conditions were statistically significant. <ModelPill model="openai" label="GPT-4o-mini" /> changed fewer decisions, 4.60% to 5.75%, and leaned the other way, toward more urgent care, though not significantly. Education alone can change an AI triage decision, and aggregate accuracy does not show it.
